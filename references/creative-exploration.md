## Explore to resolve uncertainty

Divergence is useful when the organizing idea, product character, interaction model, or composition remains uncertain. It is wasteful when applied mechanically to a narrow accessibility fix or a clearly constrained component repair.

Define the creative question before generating options:

- What kind of product should this feel like?
- Which organizing model best fits the task?
- How can density remain elegant?
- What can be ownable without harming learned behavior?
- Which convention is worth preserving, and which is worth challenging?

Set a time and fidelity budget. Produce the smallest evidence needed to choose a direction, then invest in depth rather than endless variations.

## Generate genuinely different directions

For substantial new work, create two or three directions when feasible. Each must change at least two fundamental choices:

- information organization;
- focal model;
- composition family;
- typographic voice;
- material and color logic;
- imagery or content treatment;
- navigation or interaction model;
- motion and temporal behavior;
- signature mechanism.

A light card grid, a dark card grid, and a glass card grid are not three directions. They are one premise with surface variants.

### Concept card

For each direction, record:

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Name | A descriptive name tied to the mechanism, not a fashionable adjective |
| Product truth | The fact or user need that justifies the direction |
| Design thesis | Character and intended experience |
| Organizing idea | Structure, focal model, and task behavior |
| Visual grammar | Type, color, material, imagery, composition |
| Behavioral grammar | Feedback, navigation, motion, acceleration |
| Signature | The ownable mechanism |
| Strength | What this direction does better than alternatives |
| Sacrifice | What becomes harder, less familiar, or more expensive |
| Validation | The comparison or test that would falsify it |

Do not average all concepts into one. Select a coherent winner and import only compatible mechanisms.

## Concept-generation lenses

Use several lenses to escape the first plausible answer.

### Task-led

Build the concept around the user's decision or manipulation. Let the primary task determine the canvas, chrome, density, and transitions. Useful for tools and operational products.

### Content-led

Let the actual content's rhythm, shape, and variability generate the composition. Useful for editorial, media, commerce, documentation, and data products.

### Object-led

Make the core domain object the persistent identity across views. Define its lifecycle, representation, and transitions before designing pages. Useful for creation, collaboration, records, and asset management.

### Emotion-led

Choose a precise emotional tension—calm urgency, warm precision, disciplined play—and derive every layer from it. Validate that the emotion fits the consequences of the task.

### Mechanism-led

Invent one reusable mechanism from product truth, such as evidence layering, route continuity, ledger geometry, or temporal composition. Extend it across structure and behavior.

### Constraint-inversion

Turn a perceived limitation into the organizing idea: intermittent connectivity becomes visible temporal resilience; dense data becomes exact instrumentation; small screens become focused single-object workspaces. Do not romanticize constraints that genuinely require removal.

### Adjacent-domain transfer

Study how another field solves an analogous problem: wayfinding, scientific instruments, publishing, broadcasting, maps, control rooms, or craft tools. Transfer the principle, not the costume.

### Typography-led

Give type a structural role: annotated margins, numerical alignment, editorial pacing, bilingual relationships, or expressive scale. Ensure the concept remains functional if the brand font fails to load.

### Motion-led

Define a temporal or spatial mechanism first—continuity, reversible exploration, live transformation—then build static states that remain coherent without animation.

### Anti-reference

Identify the dominant category pattern and deliberately reverse one assumption while preserving user value. Use this to discover alternatives, not to be contrarian for its own sake.

## Abstract references without copying

For each reference, move through five levels:

1. **Surface:** colors, font, radius, imagery—the least transferable level.
2. **Pattern:** component or layout arrangement.
3. **Mechanism:** how the pattern helps perception or behavior.
4. **Principle:** the context-independent relationship worth preserving.
5. **Reconstruction:** a new expression derived from the current product genome.

Example: instead of copying a command palette's appearance, abstract “expert acceleration through searchable intent,” then test whether the current product needs commands, object search, filters, or guided actions.

Collect contrasting references. If all references share one aesthetic, add an adjacent domain or counterexample before deciding.

## Escape template gravity

### Remove the usual first move

Temporarily prohibit the default: no cards, no hero, no sidebar, no gradient, no dashboard matrix, or no modal. Generate one viable alternative. The prohibition is an exploration tool, not a final rule.

### Change the unit of design

Stop designing pages. Design one object across its lifecycle, one decision across states, or one relationship across viewports. Page structure often improves when the unit changes.

### Use real extremes

Design with the hardest realistic content first: long names, missing images, dense tables, critical errors, mixed permissions, or one hundred items. Attractive extremes can produce more original systems than ideal demo content.

### Vary fidelity deliberately

A rough structural prototype can reveal a better idea than a polished mockup. Conversely, typography and motion sometimes require high-fidelity implementation before judgment. Match fidelity to the uncertainty.

### Seek productive tension

Combine two qualities that seem opposed but can strengthen each other: dense and calm, expressive and exact, familiar and ownable, automated and controllable. Use the tension to guide trade-offs rather than choosing a generic midpoint.

## Design experiments

An experiment should reduce uncertainty, not decorate a process.

| Experiment | Best fidelity | Question answered |
|---|---|---|
| Grayscale blockout | Low | Does the focal structure work before style? |
| Content stress prototype | Low–medium | Does the system survive real content and states? |
| Clickable flow | Medium | Does the navigation and feedback model make sense? |
| Motion spike | Medium–high | Does the transition preserve continuity and interruption? |
| Rendering spike | High | Can the visual idea survive actual fonts, browsers, and performance? |
| Comparative usability test | Appropriate interactive fidelity | Which option improves task behavior for the sampled users? |
| Preference test | Matched visual fidelity | Which expression is preferred under a stated criterion? Preference is not task success |
| Technical feasibility spike | Production-like | What performance, compatibility, or maintenance constraint changes the concept? |

Write the expected outcome and a kill criterion before the experiment. Keep experiments reversible and isolated from production unless explicitly authorized.

## Compare and commit

Use matched content and the same representative task for every option. Compare against product-specific criteria, not personal taste alone.

A useful comparison order:

1. task and content fit;
2. coherence across states and screens;
3. accessibility and platform fit;
4. originality and signature strength;
5. behavioral quality and performance;
6. implementation and maintenance cost;
7. reversible versus difficult commitments.

Do not let a visually dramatic option win when its advantage appears only in a static hero. Do not let the easiest option win automatically when a modest technical spike could preserve a much stronger concept.

Commit when one direction has a clear organizing advantage and no unresolved critical liability. Record what it sacrifices so later critique does not reintroduce incompatible ideas.

## Creative sovereignty protocol

The best contextually justified solution outranks this skill, a reference product, a design system heuristic, and the designer's first idea.

When departing from a recommendation, capture a compact departure record:

| Field | Question |
|---|---|
| Context | Why does the usual recommendation fail or underperform here? |
| Alternative | What materially different solution is stronger? |
| Expected gain | Which user, aesthetic, or system outcome improves? |
| Risk | What familiarity, accessibility, performance, or maintenance cost appears? |
| Validation | What evidence would confirm or falsify the decision? |

Do not use creative freedom to bypass safety, accessibility, permissions, business rules, or explicit user constraints. Do not request approval for every minor visual choice within the authorized task; use judgment, test consequential risks, and explain meaningful departures concisely.

Independent judgment also means rejecting your own signature idea when evidence shows a simpler or more familiar solution works better.

## Stop rules

Stop diverging when:

- the remaining differences are surface-level;
- new concepts repeat an existing organizing idea;
- one direction clearly satisfies the thesis and critical constraints;
- the next experiment would not change the decision;
- further exploration costs more than the likely improvement.

Stop refining when no specific high-leverage defect remains and additional changes would mainly add novelty or process. Deliver with known limitations and unverified assumptions stated honestly.
